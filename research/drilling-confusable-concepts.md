# Drilling easily confused concepts: what the learning research says

Research for [#19](https://github.com/bbartolome/quizzes/issues/19), part of map [#15](https://github.com/bbartolome/quizzes/issues/15) (Biases & Fallacies flash cards).

**Question.** What do primary sources on interleaving, discrimination (contrast) learning, retrieval practice and spacing say about drilling concepts that are easy to mix up? In particular: (1) how to choose distractors, (2) when to show contrast tips, (3) how to weight practice toward weak concepts or weak pairs, and (4) whether to include both card directions.

**How sure we are.** Each claim cites the paper it comes from. "Full text" means the claim was checked against the paper's own text. "Abstract" means it was checked only against the abstract or the bibliographic record. Most of these studies used word pairs, paintings, photos or prose passages, not definitions of biases. Applying them to bias and fallacy cards is our inference, and those inferences are labelled **Implication**.

---

## TL;DR

1. **Distractors: use similar ones.** Competitive (plausible, same-family) wrong options make learners retrieve what they know about each option to rule it out. That strengthens memory for the distractor concepts too. Random distractors can be rejected on surface cues and don't produce this benefit (Little & Bjork 2015). Interleaving also helps most when the categories are similar and easy to confuse (Brunmair & Richter 2019; Carvalho & Goldstone 2014). One cost: multiple-choice lures can be learned as false answers, and feedback cancels most of that (Roediger & Marsh 2005; Butler & Roediger 2008).
2. **Contrast tips: always show the correct answer. They matter most after an error, but don't skip them after a low-confidence correct answer. Explanations beat bare answers when the goal is transfer.** Feedback after errors does most of the work (Pashler et al. 2005). Feedback also doubles retention of correct answers given with low confidence (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2008). Explanation feedback beats correct-answer-only feedback on new inference questions (Butler, Godbole & Marsh 2013), which is our case, because the real test is spotting the bias in a new situation. Delayed feedback can beat immediate feedback (Butler, Karpicke & Roediger 2007), but immediate feedback is the simpler and safer default in a self-paced app.
3. **Weighting: favour weak concepts and the specific pairs that get confused, but never drop a concept entirely.** Keep confusable concepts next to each other in the sequence (Birnbaum et al. 2013). Letting learners drop cards they think they know slightly hurts learning (Kornell & Bjork 2008b). Medium-difficulty items repay study time better than the very hardest ones (Metcalfe & Kornell 2005). Model-driven personalised review beats generic spacing (Lindsey et al. 2014).
4. **Directions: include both.** Retrieval practice mostly strengthens the exact cue → response pairing that was practised. Transfer to rearranged cue/response (for example term → definition versus definition → term) was "minimal to none" except when learners processed detailed feedback thoroughly. The recommendation is to practise "as many cue-response combinations as possible" (Pan 2018; Pan & Rickard 2018).

## 1. Choosing distractors

### Competitive distractors drive discrimination

- **Little & Bjork (2015)**, *Memory & Cognition* 43, 14–26. Full text.
  - With *competitive* wrong alternatives, test-takers "are led to retrieve previously studied information pertaining to each alternative in order to discriminate among them and select an answer." That processing strengthens later access to information about **both the correct and the incorrect options**.
  - On a delayed cued-recall test, questions whose answers had appeared as *competitive* wrong options were answered better than control questions. When the same items had appeared as *noncompetitive* options, they were not. "Competitive alternatives are necessary for this benefit to occur."
  - The authors explain why using their planets example. If the question asks about an outer planet, Mars and Mercury can be rejected with general knowledge ("not outer planets"), so nothing specific about them is retrieved. Uranus and Saturn can't be rejected that way, so the learner has to recall specifics about each.
  - The result held with and without corrective feedback (Experiment 2).
  - Competitive alternatives were *not* more likely than noncompetitive ones to be given as wrong answers later, so the benefit isn't simply that the distractors became more familiar.
  - Note: the noncompetitive options weren't absurd. They were picked 14–17% of the time.

**Implication.** Distractors for a bias card should come from the same confusion family, for example *availability heuristic* versus *representativeness* versus *base-rate neglect*. If the tip on that card explains the discriminating feature, the card teaches all three concepts, not just the target. A random distractor (for example *straw man* on an anchoring card) can be rejected on surface category (fallacy versus bias) and teaches little.

### Interleaving pays off most for similar categories

- **Brunmair & Richter (2019)**, "Similarity matters: A meta-analysis of interleaved learning and its moderators," *Psychological Bulletin* 145(11), doi:10.1037/bul0000209. Abstract and secondary summaries; the full text's custom font encoding couldn't be extracted.
  - 59 studies, 238 effect sizes. Overall interleaving benefit was moderate (Hedges' g ≈ 0.42), and largest for paintings (g ≈ 0.67).
  - Similarity between categories is the key moderator: interleaving helps more when categories are similar and hard to tell apart. Exact moderator coefficients were not checked.
- **Carvalho & Goldstone (2014)**, *Memory & Cognition* 42(3), 481–495. Abstract.
  - The advantage of interleaved over blocked study depends on category structure. In the authors' summary (Frontiers in Psychology 2014, 5:936), interleaving helps for **high-similarity** categories by encouraging between-category comparison. Blocking helps for **low-similarity** categories by encouraging within-category comparison.
- **Rohrer (2012)**, "Interleaving helps students distinguish among similar concepts," *Educational Psychology Review* 24(3), 355–367. Abstract. A review concluding that at least part of practice should be interleaved so learners must pick the right concept, not just apply the one they were just shown. Rohrer calls the evidence "limited."

### The cost of plausible lures, and how feedback offsets it

- **Roediger & Marsh (2005)**, *JEP: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 31(5), 1155–1159. Abstract. Multiple-choice testing improved later recall overall. But reading more lures reduced the benefit and increased lure intrusions on a later test, a form of "false knowledge."
- **Butler & Roediger (2008)**, *Memory & Cognition* 36(3), 604–616. Abstract. Feedback, immediate or delayed, raised later correct answers and **reduced lure intrusions** compared with no feedback.

**Implication.** Keep the option count modest (3–4). Always reveal the correct answer, and say why the chosen lure was wrong.

## 2. When and how to show contrast tips

- **Pashler, Cepeda, Wixted & Rohrer (2005)**, *JEP: LMC* 31(1), 3–8. Full text.
  - Giving the correct answer after an **error** increased 1-week retention by 494% compared with no feedback.
  - Feedback after **correct** answers had little effect.
- **Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2008)**, *JEP: LMC* 34(4), 918–928. Abstract.
  - Feedback also **doubled retention of low-confidence correct answers**. A lucky guess on a multiple-choice card is effectively an error.
  - "The function of feedback is to correct both memory errors and metacognitive errors."
- **Butler, Godbole & Marsh (2013)**, *Journal of Educational Psychology* 105(2), 290–298. Full text (abstract and introduction).
  - Explanation feedback and correct-answer-only feedback gave equal results on *repeated* questions.
  - Explanation feedback was **better on new inference questions** two days later.
- **Butler, Karpicke & Roediger (2007)**, *JEP: Applied* 13(4), 273–281. Abstract.
  - Delayed feedback gave better final-test performance than immediate feedback, which the authors attribute to spacing.
  - Answer-until-correct feedback was no better than simply showing the correct answer.
- **Pan (2018)**, dissertation (see §4). Full text (abstract). Detailed, thoroughly processed feedback was the only thing that produced transfer to rearranged questions. In Pan's triple-associates study, an *incorrect* training response followed by feedback gave positive transfer, while a correct response gave none or even negative transfer.

**Implication.**

- Show the contrast tip **after every answer**, and make it prominent after errors.
- Name the lure the learner picked: "You picked X; X is about …, whereas Y is about …". This is explanation feedback aimed at the specific confusion.
- If the app ever records confidence, treat low-confidence correct answers like errors.
- Delaying the tip (for example, a review screen at the end of the round) is an option backed by evidence. But the delayed-feedback benefit depends on learners actually processing the delayed feedback, and an immediate tip is the safer default for a self-paced PWA where people may quit before the end of a round.
- No primary study was found that directly compares a *contrast-framed* tip with a plain explanation. That part is extrapolated.

## 3. Weighting practice toward weak concepts and weak pairs

- **Birnbaum, Kornell, Bjork & Bjork (2013)**, "Why interleaving enhances inductive learning: The roles of discrimination and retrieval," *Memory & Cognition* 41(3), 392–402. Abstract.
  - Temporal spacing was **harmful when it broke up the juxtaposition** of the interleaved categories, even with total spacing held constant.
  - Spacing still helped when it didn't interrupt discrimination.
  - So the benefit comes from seeing the confusable categories *close together*.
- **Kang & Pashler (2012)**, *Applied Cognitive Psychology* 26, 97–103. Abstract, via citing papers. Interleaving painting styles helped. Pure temporal spacing, which doesn't highlight differences between categories, did not.
- **Kornell & Bjork (2008a)**, *Psychological Science* 19(6), 585–592. Abstract.
  - Interleaving/spacing helped participants learn artists' styles.
  - Most participants nonetheless *believed* blocked (massed) study worked better.
  - So learners' sense of which concepts they "have" is unreliable.
- **Kornell & Bjork (2008b)**, "Optimising self-regulated study: The benefits—and costs—of dropping flashcards," *Memory* 16(2), 125–136. Abstract. Across four experiments, letting learners drop cards they felt they knew had **small but consistently negative effects** on learning.
- **Metcalfe & Kornell (2005)**, *Journal of Memory and Language* 52, 463–477, together with **Metcalfe & Kornell (2003)**, *JEP: General* 132, 530–542. Abstract.
  - Under the region-of-proximal-learning account, the most useful items are those just beyond what the learner currently knows.
  - Spending most of the time on the very hardest items did worse than spending it on medium-difficulty items.
- **Lindsey, Shroyer, Pashler & Mozer (2014)**, *Psychological Science* 25, 639–647. Abstract.
  - A personalised review scheduler, based on a memory model fitted to each student, gave **16.5%** better retention than massed study and **10.0%** better than one-size-fits-all spaced review on a cumulative exam after the semester.

**Implication.**

- Weight draws toward weak concepts, for example using Reasoning Traps per-trait accuracy and in-app error history. Track errors **per pair** (target, lure chosen), because a confusion is a property of the pair.
- When a pair is weak, show those two concepts **close together**: same round, with each as the other's distractor.
- Keep a floor weight so mastered concepts still appear. Never fully drop them.
- Avoid flooding a round with the hardest items. A mix weighted toward "weak but within reach" is better supported.
- Reasoning Traps accuracy is a reasonable starting point, but because people misjudge their own learning, in-app performance should take over as it accumulates.

## 4. Both card directions?

- **Pan (2018)**, *Investigations of Retrieval Practice and Transfer of Learning for Rearranged Stimulus-Response Elements*, PhD dissertation, UC San Diego (eScholarship qt45x0w7t8). Full text (abstract).
  - Four studies, including **term–definition neuroscience facts** (Study 3).
  - Retrieval practice consistently helped when practice and final-test cues and responses matched. Improvements were "substantially reduced" when the final test rearranged them, "regardless of the amount of, and the format of, retrieval practice."
  - The only exception was "detailed and extensively processed feedback."
  - Recommendation: "retrieval practice should be targeted at as many cue-response combinations as possible during training, or should incorporate extensively processed feedback."
- **Pan & Rickard (2018)**, "Transfer of test-enhanced learning: Meta-analytic review and synthesis," *Psychological Bulletin* 144(7), 710–756. Bibliographic record and secondary notes. Stimulus–response rearrangement is one of the transfer types it covers. Overall transfer was about d ≈ 0.40. Per-category figures were not checked.

**Implication.** Include both directions. *Definition/example → term* is the skill that matters in real life (recognising the bias in the wild), so weight it more heavily. *Term → definition* trains a different retrieval route and won't come for free from the other direction. Scenario/example cards can also double as the "new inference question" that explanation feedback helps with (Butler et al. 2013).

---

## Open gaps

- No primary study was found on contrast feedback that names the chosen lure versus a generic explanation. The recommendation in §2 is extrapolated from Butler et al. (2013) and Little & Bjork (2015).
- No study was found that weights by *pair*-level confusion. That idea combines Birnbaum et al. (2013), on keeping confusable items together, with error-targeted feedback (Pashler et al. 2005).
- Exact moderator coefficients from Brunmair & Richter (2019), and per-category transfer effects from Pan & Rickard (2018), were not checked against the full text.
